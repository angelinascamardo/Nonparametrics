5.1

Method

The Mann-Whitney test is also known as the Wilcoxon ranked sum test. It is used to compare two independent random samples. Let \(X_1, X_2, ..., X_n\) denote the random sample of size \(n\) from population 1. Let \(Y_1, Y_2, ..., Y_m\) denote the random sample of size \(m\) from population 2. Assign the ranks 1 to \(n+m\) to the observations from smallest to largest. Let \(R(X_i)\) and \(R(Y_j)\) be the rank assigned to \(X_i\) and \(Y_j\) for all \(i\) and \(j\). Note: \(N = n+m\). If several sample values are exactly equal to each other (tied) assign to each the average of the ranks that would have been assigned to them had there been no ties.

Assumptions

  1. Both samples are random samples from their respective populations.
  2. In addition to independence within each sample, there is mutual independence between the two samples
  3. The measurement scale is at least ordinal

Parametric equivalent (or proof that there is none)

The parametric equivalent of the Mann-Whitney test is the independent samples t-test. It is used to measure the differences/compare means of two independent samples. More can be found here: https://libguides.library.kent.edu/spss/independentttest

Hypotheses

Two-tailed:

  • \(H_0\): \(F(x) = G(x)\) for all \(x\)
  • \(H_A\): \(F(x) \neq G(x)\) for some \(x\) OR \(P(X > Y) \neq P(X<Y)\)

Left-tailed:

  • \(H_0\): \(F(x) = G(x)\) for all \(x\)
  • \(H_A\): \(F(x) > G(x)\) for some \(x\) OR \(E(X) < E(Y)\) OR \(P(X > Y) < P(X<Y)\)

Right-tailed:

  • \(H_0\): \(F(x) = G(x)\) for all \(x\)
  • \(H_A\): \(F(x) < G(x)\) for some \(x\) OR \(E(X) > E(Y)\) OR \(P(X > Y) > P(X<Y)\)

where \(F(x)\) and \(G(x)\) are the distribution functions corresponding to \(X\) and \(Y\) respectively. ## Rejection Region Two-tailed:

  • Reject \(H_0\) at a level of significance \(\alpha\) if \(T\) is less than its \(\alpha/2\) quantile or greater than its \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile obtained from Table A7 for \(n \leq 20\) and \(m \leq 20\). Otherwise obtained from table A1 (even in the case of \(T_1\))

Left-tailed:

  • Reject \(H_0\) at a level of significance \(\alpha\) if \(T\) is less than its \(\alpha/2\) quantile or greater than its \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile obtained from Table A7 for \(n \leq 20\) and \(m \leq 20\). If \(T_1\) is used, reject \(H_0\) if \(T_1 < z_\alpha\)

Right-tailed:

  • Reject \(H_0\) at a level of significance \(\alpha\) if \(T\) is less than its \(\alpha/2\) quantile or greater than its \(1-\alpha/2\) quantile obtained from Table A7 for \(n \leq 20\) and \(m \leq 20\). If \(T_1\) is used, reject \(H_0\) if \(T_1 > z_{\alpha-1}\)

Test Statistic and/or p-value

  • Test Statistic: If there are no/few ties, the sum of the ranks assigned to the sample from population 1 can be used as a test statistic. \[ T = \sum_{i=1}^n R(X_i) \] If there are many ties, subtract the mean from \(T\) and divide by the standard deviation to get:

\[ T_1 = \frac{T-n \frac{N+1}{2}}{\sqrt{\frac{nm}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i=1}^N R_i^2- \frac{nm(N+1)^2}{4(N-1)}}} \] where \(\sum R_i^2\) refers to the sum of the squares of all \(N\) of the ranks or average ranks actually used in both samples.

  • The approximate quantiles are found using the normal approximation in the case of no ties, and \(n\) or \(m>20\). If there are many ties, then \(T_1\) is used instead of \(T\) and \(T_1\) is approximately a standard normal random variable whose quanitles are given in Table A1.
  • The p-value is obtained differently for each different tailed test:
    • Two-tailed: In the case of \(T\), substitute \(T\) or \(T'\) into the following equation:

\[ 2*P(Z \leq \frac{T+ \frac{1}{2} - n \frac{N+1}{2}}{\sqrt{\frac{nm(N+1)}{12}}}) \] where \(Z\) is a standard normal random variable. In the case of \(T_1\), the p-value is twice the smaller of \(P(Z \ leq T_1)\) or \(P(Z \geq T_1)\) - Left-tailed: similarly to the equation above, but without multiplying it by 2. In the case of \(T_1\) the p-value is approximately \(P(Z \leq T_1\) obtained from Table A1. - Right-tailed: similarly to the equation above, but without multiplying it by 2. If \(T_1\) is used, p-value = \(P(Z \geq T_1) = 1-P(Z \leq T_1)\) obtained from Table A1.

Confidence Interval

Data

The data consists of two random samples \(X_1, ..., X_n\) and \(Y_1,...,Y_m\) of size \(n\) and \(m\). Let \(X\) and \(Y\) denote random variables with the same distribution as the two random samples.

Assumptions

  1. Both samples are random samples from their respective populations
  2. Independence within each sample, and mutual independence between the two samples
  3. The two population distribution functions are identical except for a possible difference in location parameters. I.e. there is a constant \(d\) such that \(X\) has the same distribution function as \(Y+d\).

Method

Find the \(\alpha/2\) quantile \(w_{\alpha/2}\) for \(n\) and \(m\) from Table A7 if \(n\) and \(m\) are large, where \((1-\alpha)\) is the confidence coefficient. Then calculate \(k\) using: \[ k = w_{\alpha/2} - n(n+1)/2 \] From all the possible pairs \((X_i, Y_j)\) find the \(k\) largest differences \(X_i-Y_j\) and the smallest differences. Ordering each sample from smallest to largest first is convenient, then form a matrix of differences using Xs as rows and Ys as columns. The largest difference is the upper limit \(U\) and the smallest difference is the lower limit \(L\). Then the confidence interval is: \[ P[L \leq E(X) - E(Y) \leq U] \geq 1-\alpha \]

R function for Mann-Whitney/Wilcoxon rank-sum test (obtained from https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/stats/versions/3.6.2/topics/wilcox.test)

  • \(H_A\): \(M_{x} > M{y}\)

5.2

Method

The Kruskall-Wallis test determines if \(k\) independent samples come from populations with the same distribution.

Assumptions

  1. All samples are random samples from their respective populations
  2. Independence within each sample and mutual independence among the various samples
  3. The measurement scale is at least ordinal
  4. Either the \(k\) population distribution functions are identical, or else some of the populations tend to yield larger values than other populations do

Parametric equivalent (or proof that there is none)

The parametric equivalent of the Kruskall-Wallis test is the one way ANOVA test. ANOVA tests for equal variance between groups and normal distribution.

Note: I have previously learned ANOVA and Kruskall-Wallis in a Biostatistics course, so I have extensive notes already of these tests.

Hypotheses

  • \(H_0\): \(M_1 = ... = M_k\)
  • \(H_A\): at least one \(M_i\) is different

Rejection Region

Reject \(H_0\) if \(\chi_0^2\) is greater than its \(1-\alpha\) quantile from the null distribution.

Test Statistic and/or p-value

  • Test Statistic: Let \(p_j^*\) be the probability of a random observation on \(X\) being in class \(j\), under the assumption that the null hypothesis is true. \[ \chi_0^2 = \frac{12}{n(n+1) \sum_{i =1}^k \frac{R_i^2}{n_i}}-3 (n+1) \] where \(R_i\) is the sum of the ranks for group \(i\), \(n_i\) is the sample size for group \(i\), \(n = \sum_{i=1}^k n_i\) is the total sample size, and \(k\) is the number of groups.

  • The chi squared distribution with \(k-1\) degrees of freedom is used as an approximation to the null distribution of \(\chi_0^2\).

  • The p-value is approximately the probability of chi squared random variable with \(k-1\) degrees of freedom exceeding the observed value of \(\chi_0^2\). \[ p = P[\chi_{k-1}^2 \geq \chi_0^2] \]

R function

R function for Kruskal-Wallis test (obtained from https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/stats/versions/3.6.2/topics/kruskal.test)

kruskal.test(continuous_variable ~ grouping_variable, data = dataset_name)
