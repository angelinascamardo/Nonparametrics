3.3

Method

Tolerance limits are different from confidence intervals in that they provide an interval within which at least a proportion \(q\) of the population lies, within probability \(1-\alpha\) or more that the stated interval does indeed “contain” the proportion \(q\) of the population.

With a sample size of \(n\), there is a probability that at least \(1-\alpha\) that at least \(q\) [or \((100)(q)%\)] of the population is between \(X^{(r)}\) and \(X^{(n+1-m)}\) inclusive. For one-sided tolerance regions let either \(r\) or \(m = 0\), where \(X^{(r)}\) and \(X^{(n+1)}\) are considered to be \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\) respectively, and then proceed with methods.

Data

The data consists of a random sample \(X_1, X_2, ..., X_n\) from a larger population. A confidence coefficient is chosen, \(1-\alpha\) and a pair of integers \(r\) and \(m\) are chosen as well.

Important Note! We try to make the statement: “The probability is \(1-\alpha\) that the random interval from \(X^{(r)}\) to \(X^{(n+1-m)}\) inclusive contains a proportion \(q\) or more of the population.”

Assumptions

  1. The \(X_1, X_2, ..., X_n\) are a random sample.
  2. The measurement scale is at least ordinal.

Approach

Approach A (to find \(n\)):

  • Used when we want to determine the required sample size \(n\) after selecting a desired population proportion \(q\)
  • If \(r+m = 1\), or if either \(r\) or \(m = 0\) as a one-sided tolerance limit, read \(n\) directly from Table A5 to find the appropriate values of \(\alpha\) and \(q\).
  • If \(r+m=2\), read \(n\) from Table A6 for the appropriate values of \(\alpha\) and \(q\).
  • If Table A5 and A6 are not appropriate, use the approximation:

\[ n \cong \frac 14 x_{1-\alpha} \frac {1+q} {1-q} + \frac 12 (r+m-1) \] where \(x_{1-\alpha}\) is the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of a chi-squared random variable with \(2(r+m)\) degrees of freedom, obtained from Table A2.

Approach B (to find \(q\)):

  • Used when we want to determine the population proportion \(q\) for a given sample size \(n\)
  • For a given sample size \(n\) and selected values of \(\alpha\), \(r\), and \(m\), the approximate value of \(q\), the proportion of the population, is given by:

\[ q = \frac {4n-2(r+m-1)-x_{1-\alpha}} {4n-2(r+m-1)+x_{1-\alpha}} \]

  2. At least what percent of its members have fat percentage between \(X^{(2)}\) and \(X^{(85)}\) with 95% certainty? With 90% certainty?

# part (a): [X(1), X(86)]
p95 <- 1 - 0.05^(1/86)

With 95% confidence, at least 92.86% of the population have fat percentage between \(X^{(2)}\) and \(X^{(85)}\). With 90% confidence, at least 93.93% of the population have fat percentage between \(X^{(2)}\) and \(X^{(85)}\).

3.4

Method

The sign test is the more simple version of the binomial test with \(p^* = 1/2 = 1 - p^*\). The sign test is useful when trying to find out whether one random variable in a pair \((X, Y)\) is larger than the other random variable in the pair. The data will consist of observations on a bivariate random sample \((X_1, Y_1), (X_2, Y_2), ... , (X_n, Y_n)\) where there are \(n'\) pairs of observations. With each pair, a comparison is made, and the comparison is then classified as either “+” (plus, if \(X_i < Y_i\)) or “-” (minus, if \(X_i > Y_i\)) or “0” (tie, if \(X_i = Y_i\)).

Assumptions

  1. The bivariate random variables \((X_i, Y_i), i = 1, 2, ..., n'\) are mutually independent.
  2. The measurement scale is at least ordinal within each pair (i.e. each can be classified as plus, minus, or tie).
  3. Pairs are internally consistent (i.e. if \(P(+) > P(-)\) for one pair, it is for all pairs).

Parametric equivalent (or proof that there is none)

  • The Wilcoxon signed rank test is the parametric equivalent if we assume the difference \(Y_i - X_i\) are random variables with a symmetric distribution function (i.e. the distribution function of a random variable \(Z\) is symmetric about some point \(c\) if \(P(Z \leq c - x) = P(Z \geq c + x)\) for all \(x\)).
  • The paired \(t\)-test is the parametric equivalent if the differences \(Y_i - X_i\) are independent and identically distributed normal random variables.

Hypotheses

  • \(H_0\): \(P(+) = P(-)\) (two-tailed) | \(P(+) \geq P(-)\) (left-tailed) | \(P(+) \leq P(-)\) (right-tailed)
  • \(H_A\): \(P(+) \neq P(-)\) (two-tailed) | \(P(+) < P(-)\) (left-tailed) | \(P(+) > P(-)\) (right-tailed)

In terms of null distribution, it could be interpreted in the following ways:

  • Two-tailed:

    • \(H_0\): \(E(X_i) = E(Y_i)\) for all i (for means) | \(M\) of \(X_i = M\) of \(Y_i\) for all i (for medians)
    • \(H_A\): \(E(X_i) \neq E(Y_i)\) for all i | \(M\) of \(X_i \neq M\) of \(Y_i\) for all i

  • Left-tailed:

    • \(H_0\): \(E(X_i) \leq E(Y_i)\) for all i (for means) | \(M\) of \(X_i \leq M\) of \(Y_i\) for all i (for medians)
    • \(H_A\): \(E(X_i) > E(Y_i)\) for all i | \(M\) of \(X_i > M\) of \(Y_i\) for all i

  • Right-tailed:

    • \(H_0\): \(E(X_i) \geq E(Y_i)\) for all i (for means) | \(M\) of \(X_i \geq M\) of \(Y_i\) for all i (for medians)
    • \(H_A\): \(E(X_i) < E(Y_i)\) for all i | \(M\) of \(X_i < M\) of \(Y_i\) for all i

Rejection Region

Two-tailed:

  • For \(n \leq 20\), use Table A3 with \(n\) and \(p=1/2\); find about \(\alpha/2\) and call it \(\alpha_1\). The value of \(y\) corresponding to \(\alpha_1\) is \(t\).
  • Reject \(H_0\) if \(T \leq t\) or if \(T \geq n - t\) at a level of significance of \(2\alpha_1\)
  • For \(n > 20\), use the normal approximation to get: \[ t = \frac{1}{2} (n + z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{n}) \] where \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is obtained from Table A1. And, if \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(z_{\alpha/2} = (-1.96)\), then: \[ t = \frac{n}{2} - \sqrt{n} \]

Left-tailed:

  • Note: small values of \(T\) indicate that a minus is more probable than a plus (agreeing with \(H_1\))
  • For \(n \leq 20\), use Table A3 with \(n\) and \(p=1/2\); find about \(\alpha\) and call it \(\alpha_1\). The value of \(y\) corresponding to \(\alpha_1\) is \(t\).
  • For \(n > 20\), \(t\) is found using this approximation: \[ t = \frac{1}{2} (n + z_{\alpha} \sqrt{n}) \]
  • Reject \(H_0\) if \(T \leq t\) at a significance level of \(\alpha_1\) or \(\alpha\).

Right-tailed:

  • Note: big values of \(T\) indicate that a plus is more probable than a minus (agreeing with \(H_1\))
  • For \(n \leq 20\), use Table A3 with \(n\) and \(p=1/2\); find about \(\alpha\) and call it \(\alpha_1\). The value of \(y\) corresponding to \(\alpha_1\) is \(t\). (same as left-tailed test)
  • For \(n > 20\), \(t\) is found using the same approximation as the left-tailed test.

Test Statistic and/or p-value

  • Test Statistic: \(T\) is the number of “plus” pairs (i.e. \(T=\) number of pairs \((X_i, Y_i)\) where \(X_i < Y_i\)).
  • The null distribution of \(T\) is the binomial distribution with \(p = 1/2\) and \(n=\) number of noted pairs (i.e. disregard all tied pairs \(X=Y\) and let \(n=\) total number of pluses and minuses). Normal approximation is used for \(n>20\).
  • The p-value is obtained differently for the different forms:
    • Two-tailed: p-value is twice the smaller of the probabilities that \(Y \leq\) or \(\geq\) the observed value of \(T\) (found from Table A3 for \(n \leq 20\), Table A1 if \(n > 20\))
    • Left-tailed: p-value is the probability that \(Y \leq\) observed value of \(T\)
    • Right-tailed: p-value is the probability that \(Y \geq\) observed value of \(T\)

R function

R function for sign test (obtained from https://people.stat.sc.edu/hitchcock/Rexamples518section3_4.txt)

sign.test<-function(x=0,y=NULL,alternative="two.sided"){
  n<-sum((x-y)!=0)
  T<-sum(x<y)
  if (alternative=="less") {
    p.value<-pbinom(T,n,0.5)}
  if (alternative=="greater"){
    p.value<- 1-pbinom(T-1,n,0.5)}
  if (alternative=="two.sided"){
    p.value<-2*min(1-pbinom(T-1,n,0.5),pbinom(T,n,0.5))}
  list(n=n,alternative=alternative,T=T,p.value=p.value)}

3.5

Method

The McNemar test aims to answer the question: “Can we detect a difference between the probability of (0,1) and the probability of (1,0)?” This is a different version of the sign test where the data is on a nominal measurement scale with categories “0” and “1”. The data will consist of observations on \(n'\) independent bivariate random variables \((X_i, Y_i), i = 1, 2, ..., n'\).

The data is usually organized into a contingency table, where \(a\) = the number of pairs where \(X_i = 0\) and \(Y_i = 0\); \(b\) = the number of pairs where \(X_i = 0\) and \(Y_i = 1\); \(c\) = the number of pairs where \(X_i = 1\) and \(Y_i = 0\); and \(d\) = the number of pairs where \(X_i = 1\) and \(Y_i = 1\).

Assumptions

  1. The pairs \((X_i, Y_i)\) are mutually independent.
  2. The measurement scale is nominal with two categories “0” and “1” for all \(X_i\) and \(Y_i\)
  3. The difference \(P(X_i = 0, Y_i = 1) - P(X_i = 1, Y_i = 0)\) is negative/zero/positive for all \(i\)

Parametric equivalent (or proof that there is none)

The McNemar test is equivalent to the paired sample t-tests but for dichotomous dependent variables, as opposed to for continuous dependent variables by the paired sample tests (via https://real-statistics.com/non-parametric-tests/mcnemars-test/).

Hypotheses

  • \(H_0\): \(P(X_i = 0, Y_i = 1) = P(X_i = 1, Y_i = 0)\) for all \(i\)
  • \(H_A\): \(P(X_i = 0, Y_i = 1) \neq P(X_i = 1, Y_i = 0)\) for all \(i\)

These hypotheses can also be interpreted like this, if we add \(P(X_i = 0, Y_i = 0)\) to both sides:

  • \(H_0\): \(P(X_i = 0) = P(Y_i = 0)\) for all \(i\) | \(P(X_i = 1) = P(Y_i = 1)\) for all \(i\)
  • \(H_A\): \(P(X_i = 0) \neq P(Y_i = 0)\) for all \(i\) | \(P(X_i = 1) \neq P(Y_i = 1)\) for all \(i\)

Rejection Region

  • Let \(n=b+c\). If \(n \leq 20\), use Table A3. Enter table with \(p=1/2\) and \(n=b+c\) to find approximately \(\alpha/2\) (called \(\alpha_1\)). The corresponding value of \(y\) is \(t\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(T_2 \leq t\) or if \(T_2 \geq n - t\) at a significance level of \(2\alpha_1\).
  • If \(n>20\), use \(T_1\) and Table A2. Reject \(H_0\) at a level of significance \(\alpha\) if \(T_1\) exceeds the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of a chi-squared random variable with 1 degree of freedom.

Test Statistic and/or p-value

  • Test Statistic:
    • For \(b+c > 20\): \(T_1 = \frac{(b-c)^2}{b+c}\)
    • For \(b+c \leq 20\): \(T_2 = b\)
  • The null distribution of \(T_1\) is approximately the chi-squared distribution with 1 degree of freedom when \((b+c)\) is large. The exact distribution of \(T_2\) is the binomial distribution with \(p=1/2\) and \(n=b+c\).
  • For \(n \leq 20\), the p-value is twice the probability of \(T_2 \leq\) \(\geq\) the observed value (whichever is smaller). For \(n>20\), the p-value is the probability of \(T_1\) exceeding the observed value (as found in Table A2 for the chi-squared distribution with 1 degree of freedom).

R function

R function for McNemar test (obtained from https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/stats/versions/3.6.2/topics/mcnemar.test)

mcnemar.test(x, y = NULL, correct = TRUE)

where x is either a two-dimensional contingency table in matrix form or a factor object, y is a factor object (it will be ignored if x is a matrix), and correct indicates whether or not to apply continuity correction when computing the test statistic.

Method

The Cox and Stuart test for trend involves pairing the later numbers with the earlier numbers and performing a sign test on the pairs. A trend is present if one member of each pair has a tendency to be higher/lower than the other member. A trend is not present if the sequence of numbers actually represents observations on independent and identically distributed random variables (i.e. no tendency for one member to exceed the other one). It can also be used to detect any specified type of nonrandom pattern (i.e. sine wave, periodic pattern).

NOTE: A sequence of numbers is said to have a trend if the later numbers in the sequence tend to be greater than the earlier numbers (upward trend) or less than the earlier numbers (downward trend).

The data will consist of observations on a sequence of random variables \(X_1, X_2, ..., X_{n'}\), arranged in a particular order. Group the random variables into pairs \((X_1, X_{1+c}), (X_2, X_{2+c}), ..., (X_{n'-c}, X_{n'})\), where \(c = n'/2\) if \(n'\) is even, otherwise \(c=(n'+1)/2\). Replace each pair \((X_i, X_{i+c})\) with a “+” if \(X_i < X_{i+c}\) or a “-” if \(X_i > X_{i+c}\). Eliminate ties. The number of untied pairs is called \(n\).

Assumptions

  1. The random variables \(X_1, X_2, ..., X_{n'}\) are mutually independent
  2. The measurement scale of the \(X_i\)s is at least ordinal
  3. Either the \(X_i\)s are identically distributed or there is a trend; meaning that the random variables are more likely to be greater than instead of less than the earlier random variables (or vice versa).

Parametric equivalent (or proof that there is none)

There is no direct parametric equivalent that I am able to find.

Hypotheses

The hypotheses are the same as the sign test. The null hypothesis is that no trend is present. A right-tailed test is used to detect an upward trend, and a left-tailed test is used to detect a downward trend. The two-tailed test is used if the alternative hypothesis is that any type of trend (upward or downward) exists.

Rejection Region

The rejection region is the same as the sign test.

Test Statistic and/or p-value

  • Test Statistic: \(T\) = total number of +’s
  • The null distribution of \(T\) is approximately the binomial distribution with \(p=1/2\) and \(n=\) number of untied pairs, where \(X_i \neq X_{i+c}\).
  • The p-value is calculated exactly as in the sign test.
